# Presenter notes - Manager session 7: Beating the bias

Audience: middle management and team leads. The room will feel accused unless you frame this as an
instrument-calibration problem. The rater is the instrument; instruments get calibrated, not blamed.

## Before the session
- Have the citation and the figure ready: Scullen, Mount and Goff (2000), idiosyncratic rater
  effects reported at roughly 53 to 62 percent of variance depending on rater level. Someone will
  ask, and hand-waving loses the session.
- Same for the language findings: Correll and Simard (HBR 2016) on vague feedback and the
  specificity gap, and the 2014 review-language analysis on personality criticism. Say "reported."
- If you can, count evidence-backed statements versus adjectives across your OWN last review
  cycle, per person, and bring the result. Going first is what makes the room willing to look.
- Know your own set point. If you are a generous rater, say so out loud early.

## Timing (45 min)
- 0-3: Recap - the next leak after separating the conversations
- 3-14: Part 1 - the idiosyncratic rater effect; four distortions; contrast and similarity
- 14-20: Part 2 - what the review language gives away
- 20-32: Worked example 1 - audit a Cartwheel review cycle's language
- 32-42: Worked example 2 - run a fair calibration session, plus the self-audit
- 42-45: Q&A

## Worked-example run-of-show
- Open Part 1 on the variance bar: about a fifth of the variance is the person being rated, over
  half is the individual rater. Let that sit before you soften it. Then soften it precisely: real
  differences exist, they are just a smaller share than we assume, and the fix is structural.
- The two-managers-one-analyst example (a 4 from a manager who rates everyone 4, a 3 from one who
  reserves 4 for exceptional) is the concrete version. Nothing about her work changed.
- Worked example 1: run the four review lines and let the room name the pattern before the table.
  The beat to land: every rewrite becomes contestable. A person can argue with evidence.
- Worked example 2: the five calibration rules, then the blunt test - if the session's output is a
  rank order, it was a ranking meeting. If it is a shared evidenced standard plus a few adjusted
  ratings with written reasons, it was calibration.
- Close on the four-question self-audit. Have everyone answer it about one real rating, silently.

## Watch for
- "So ratings are worthless." Point at the self-study card: removing the visible rating moves the
  judgment somewhere less auditable, which is not less biased.
- The quota defence: "we have to fit a distribution." Separate the two decisions out loud -
  calibration levels set points, budget allocation is a different, clearly-named step.
- Managers auditing their language and finding no problem in five seconds. Ask for counts, not
  impressions. This is the same evidence rule applied to themselves.
- Anyone concluding that vague praise is harmless because the score was generous. Vague praise is
  a career tax; that is the whole point of Part 2.

## Cuts if long
- Contrast and similarity bias drop to self-study.
- Worked example 2 compresses to the five rules plus the ranking-versus-calibration test.
- Never cut the variance bar or the self-audit. One is the evidence, the other is the only part
  that changes a rating they submit next month.
