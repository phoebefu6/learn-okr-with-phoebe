# Presenter notes - Manager session 8: AI-assisted, responsibly

Audience: middle management and team leads. Practical, not legal. They want to know what they may
use it for on Monday, and the answer is the two-lane rule.

## Before the session
- Re-verify the regulation specifics the week you deliver: EU AI Act Annex III status and dates,
  and NYC Local Law 144 details. Present them as of a date, and say once that this is awareness,
  not legal advice.
- Find out which AI tools your organisation has actually approved for personal data. The
  where-does-the-data-go question is the one that gets asked, and "check with IT" is a weak answer
  if you could have checked.
- Have the two cautionary cases attributed properly: Microsoft Productivity Score (2020) and the
  subsequent removal of individual-level identification; Amazon warehouse "time off task" as
  reported by MIT Technology Review.
- Decide whether you will show a real AI-drafted review. If you do, use your own, not a report's.

## Timing (45 min)
- 0-3: Recap - AI is tempting precisely because human rating is noisy
- 3-14: Part 1 - the two lanes; three uses that genuinely reduce bias
- 14-20: Part 2 - four guardrails and the legal floor
- 20-34: Worked example 1 - build a Cartwheel review pack with AI
- 34-42: Worked example 2 - the never-automate list and the disclosure
- 42-45: Q&A

## Worked-example run-of-show
- Part 1: give the one-line rule before anything else - AI works on the evidence, the human makes
  the judgment - and repeat it at each lane. Then connect each assist use to a bias from b7:
  evidence assembly beats recency, language flagging beats the specificity gap, a draft beats the
  blank page where impressions live.
- Part 2 beat that matters most: human-in-the-loop means a decision, not a signature. Tell the
  four-minute review round story - fourteen AI drafts skimmed and submitted, two subtly wrong, one
  crediting someone else's work. The tool did not fail; the missing step did.
- Worked example 1: run the who-does-what table as a vote per row before revealing. Rows people
  get wrong are usually "draft the narrative" (AI first pass, you own it) and "score the OKR"
  (never AI, and scoring is a conversation, not an output).
- Worked example 2: the disclosure exercise has a self-correcting property worth naming - the team
  lead who got as far as "I use AI to summarise your check-in notes" and realised her reports had
  never been told. Anything you would not want to disclose is the thing to stop doing.

## Watch for
- Enthusiasm for activity data because it is easy to collect and AI makes it easy to score. That
  is the surveillance lane wearing better tooling.
- "The vendor says it is compliant." Ask for the bias-audit results and the data behind them.
- Pasting performance notes into an unapproved tool. Raise the data question explicitly; these are
  sensitive notes about identifiable people.
- The never-automate list drifting into product names. It must be a list of decisions.

## Cuts if long
- The bias-audit self-study card drops.
- Worked example 2 can produce just the never-automate list, with the disclosure as homework.
- Never cut the two-lane rule or the human-in-the-loop-is-not-a-signature beat. Those are the two
  things that keep them on the right side of the line.
